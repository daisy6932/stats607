# Critical analysis: Storey (2002), Section 5

## Reproduction and discrepancies

This study reproduces the main Table 2 pattern using 1000 replicates per condition. Fixed-region power is approximately 0.372 for gamma=0.01 and 0.138 for gamma=0.001, consistent with the normal-distribution calculation. BH power generally increases with the null proportion. Original rounded values and all reproduction differences are retained in `results/table2_comparison.csv`.

The largest surprise was the BH result at pi0=0.8 and gamma=0.001. Table 2 reports BH power of 0.129; my main run gives 0.1127 (MCSE 0.0009). A follow-up with seed 6072027 and 10,000 replicates per design gave 0.11347 with fixed counts and 0.11334 with random labels (MCSE 0.00027 and 0.00028). The gap persisted under both designs. I return to that discrepancy below. The follow-up uses [config/followup.json](config/followup.json).

## Neutrality and what the figures reveal

Storey feeds the estimated FDR for a fixed rejection region into BH on the same dataset. At pi0=0.1 and gamma=0.01, the fixed region yields empirical FDR 0.00299, compared with 0.00040 for BH. The power gain comes with more false discoveries per rejection on average. This comparison therefore cannot establish which method has higher power at the same actual FDR.

The design is favorable to tail-based null-proportion estimation: independent tests, uniform null p-values, and a common, fairly strong positive normal alternative. Table 2 compares against the original BH procedure. The adaptive method of [Benjamini and Hochberg (2000)](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986025001060) would offer another comparison because it also estimates the number of true nulls. Dependence, weak or heterogeneous signals, two-sided tests, and invalid null p-values are absent. Those omissions matter in genomics, where correlated measurements and varied effect sizes are common.

The new power plot reproduces the paper's Figure 1 concept while overlaying original points and Monte Carlo intervals. The bias diagnostic exposes residual contamination of large p-values by alternatives. At pi0=0.1, the estimated null proportion is about 0.041 too high despite close agreement with the original table. The empirical-FDR plot makes unequal error rates visible, which a power-only display cannot show.

## Confidence, implementation and next steps

The part of the implementation I found hardest to get right was the level passed to BH, because a wrong choice does not fail. Section 5 gives BH the FDR estimate from the same replicate. The pFDR estimate divides that number by 1 − (1 − gamma)^m. At gamma=0.01 this factor is essentially one, so the two estimates agree and half of Table 2 looks correct either way. At gamma=0.001 the factor is 0.632 and the pFDR estimate is about 58% larger. I settled the question with the estimated-FDR column that Table 2 prints: at pi0=0.8 and gamma=0.001 the paper reports 0.030 and my FDR estimate averages 0.0297, while my pFDR estimate averages 0.047. A unit test with four p-values, computed by hand, now keeps the two formulas apart. What I took from this is to check every printed column, not only the headline one, and to test at a setting where the candidate interpretations give different answers.

I am confident in the implementation under the stated design. Fixed-region power matches its closed-form value, the FDR benchmark matches an exact enumeration on a small example, and every BH power entry except one is within 0.003 of the printed value. The follow-up gives BH power near 0.113 under both label designs, making Monte Carlo noise in my run an unlikely explanation. The paper reports a mean BH cutoff of 0.0006 for that row, close to mine (0.00061). The theoretical power at a fixed cutoff of 0.0006 is 0.108, closer to my simulated BH power than to the reported 0.129. BH uses a random cutoff, so this calculation is a check on the scale of the result. The cutoff agreement makes a misprint plausible, though the original code would help resolve the discrepancy. Two things could still undermine my confidence: the code and its tests rest on the same reading of the paper, so a shared misreading could pass the tests, and the results say nothing about correlated tests or weaker signals.

A stronger design would calibrate each method's threshold on separate simulations, then compare power at the same achieved FDR on fresh replicates. That would separate power gains from differences in error rates. I would add correlated tests and weaker, heterogeneous signals to check whether the comparison holds beyond the original design. More replicates would improve precision for rare false discoveries.
